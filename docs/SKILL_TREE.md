# Skill & Knowledge Tree — Humanity from Nothing to the 1600s

> **Status: DESIGN DRAFT — pending owner review. Nothing in the game uses this yet.**
> Recorded 2026-08-25. Context: the owner is considering converting the game into a more
> **research & knowledge based game** where the tribe starts from nothing and discovers
> everything on the way. This document is the raw material: a detailed tree of the skills
> and knowledge humanity actually accumulated from the Paleolithic up to roughly **1650 AD**
> (tiers T0–T6; the timeline was later extended with T7–T8 and epilogue titles T9–T11 — see the
> tier table). It is deliberately larger than any game needs — pruning, merging, and costing come
> later, together with the owner.

**How to read this document**

- The tree is organized as **10 branches** (domains). Inside each branch, indentation = "builds on the node above"; cross-branch prerequisites are written as *(needs: …)*.
- Every node carries an **era tier**:

| Tier | Era | Rough real-world dates |
|---|---|---|
| **T0** | Hello World! | what the tribe begins with |
| **T1** | Bloody Roots (Paleolithic) | before ~10,000 BC |
| **T2** | Domesticated (Neolithic) | ~10,000–3500 BC |
| **T3** | Civilization! (Bronze Age) | ~3500–1200 BC |
| **T4** | Iron Age, Golden Price (Classical) | ~1200 BC–500 AD |
| **T5** | Shepherds & Sheep (Medieval) | ~500–1450 AD |
| **T6** | The Kindling (Renaissance & Discovery) | ~1450–1650 AD |
| **T7** | Golden Age, Iron Price (Age of Discovery) | ~1650–1800 AD |
| **T8** | Full Metal Century (Napoleonic Era → end of WW2) | ~1800–1945 AD |
| **Epilogue** | Are We There Yet, Aristo? (Democracy & Cold War → Technology → AI) | 1945– |

> **Timeline extension (owner, 2026-08-26):** the tree now reaches past 1650. **T7 and T8** are real
> game tiers — their era intros are written, but their **skill nodes are not**. The **Epilogue**
> ("Are We There Yet, Aristo?", covering Democracy & Cold War → Technology → AI) closes the story
> after T8; it carries **no skill nodes**.

- Nodes marked **⭐** are natural "gateway" techs — big unlocks that open several branches at once (good candidates for expensive, era-defining research in the game).

**Era ladder at a glance**

```
T0 Hello World! ──► T1 Bloody Roots ──► T2 Domesticated ──► T3 Civilization!
                (fire, tools,        (farming, herds,     (bronze, wheel,
                 hunting, speech)     pottery, weaving)    writing, cities)
──► T4 Iron Age, Golden Price ──► T5 Shepherds & Sheep ──► T6 The Kindling
     (iron, coinage,          (mills, castles,   (printing, oceanic ships,
      geometry, roads)         compass, gunpowder) anatomy, telescope)
──► T7 Golden Age, Iron Price ──► T8 Full Metal Century     (nodes TBD)
──► Epilogue: Are We There Yet, Aristo?   (1945–today · no skill nodes)
```

**Tone note (owner instruction, 2026-08-25):** all player-facing text in the game — era intros, building
and tech descriptions, events — will be written in a **humorous, black-humorous, almost-insulting** voice.
The aim is not the insult itself: the game should make the player feel that the more they face their
ancestral instincts, the further they drift from the virtues of humanity — becoming no different from
animals. The era introductions below (T0, T1, …) are the reference for this voice. Node descriptions in
this document are still neutral design notes; they get their in-game rewrite when the tree is pruned and
implemented.

## T0 — Hello World! (game introduction, first stage)

> **Hello World!**
> Human is a rational animal, says Aristo. You are not there yet...
>
> What you are, right now, is a damp huddle of upright mammals with an ambitious agenda: eat, sleep,
> and don't get eaten. Congratulations — that is the exact same agenda as the wolves watching you from
> the treeline, the crows waiting politely behind them, and the worms who are, frankly, the most patient
> of the lot. You do it all a *little* smarter, yes. You have thumbs. You have complaints. The wolves
> would trade neither for their teeth.
>
> Take an honest look at your achievements so far. Your fire? Found, not made — you keep it alive like
> a pet you don't understand, and when it dies you will sit in the dark thinking about what you've done.
> Your shelter is a pile of brush. Dinner is whatever died recently enough. And those noble human
> virtues you're so sure separate you from the beasts — love, courage, loyalty? For now they are
> pair-bonding, adrenaline, and pack instinct. Don't feel insulted. The wolves don't.
>
> So here is the deal, animal. Develop. Learn. Discover. Every skill you master is one small step away
> from the beast — though do watch, on your way up, how much of the beast you pack for the journey.
> Or don't develop, and die, and be scavenged by other animals... much like yourself. The forest is
> fine with either outcome. Welcome to the world — it wasn't waiting for you.

## T1 — Bloody Roots (era introduction)

> **Bloody Roots**
> Ah, the ancestors. One day you will tell magnificent stories about this era — the great hunts, the
> taming of fire, the grandmother who could read a broken twig like a letter. Let us be precise about
> what those legends describe: you learned to rub sticks together until they smoked, to throw a sharp
> stick slightly harder than before, and to study animal droppings with the devotion of a scholar.
> That last one is called Tracking. It is a proud family tradition. It is also, and there is no
> polite way around this, a career in droppings.
>
> Was it hard? Enormously. Half your children won't see a fifth winter, dinner regularly fights back
> and sometimes wins, and "medicine" is a leaf your aunt is fairly confident about. Respect the
> difficulty — but don't confuse difficulty with virtue. The salmon has it too, thrashing
> upstream to spawn and die, and you don't sing songs about the salmon. You eat the salmon. So does
> the bear, who also survived the same brutal winter you did, without inventing a single legend
> about it.
>
> And look closely at what these hallowed skills actually are: sharper ways to kill, cleverer ways to
> trap, patience enough to run a deer to death — the wolf's trick, minus the fur. The fire that will
> one day light libraries is currently charring a squirrel. Master these bloody roots all the same,
> for everything human will grow out of them. Just remember, when you beat your chest about your
> mighty forebears: chest-beating is the other apes' tradition too.

---

## T2 — Domesticated (era introduction)

> **Domesticated**
> At last — domestication! And no, not the sheep. The sheep barely noticed. The animal you finally
> tamed, after a hundred thousand restless years, is you. You fenced the grass, roofed the mud,
> buried your wandering feet under a floor, and named the whole arrangement "home." 
>
> To be fair, the perks are real. You can build houses now — mud, yes, but mud with a *door*, and
> a door is a wall's way of pretending you have a choice. You can hold a profession: potter, weaver,
> farmer — the same ape, sorted by chore, and enormously proud of the sorting. You can raise a
> family in one place, know your neighbors by name, and quarrel with them about fences: behold,
> society. And the granary! The granary means tomorrow finally exists. It also means everything
> worth stealing now sits in one convenient building... but let tomorrow's visitors worry about that.
>
> So stand in your doorway, survey the field you serve... pardon, *OWN*, and receive the good news:
> you are officially promoted from ape to primate. Congratulations. The ceremony is held daily, at
> dawn, in the field; bring water. For that is the small print of settling in: the sheep obey the
> shepherd, and you obey the harvest. And the harvest doesn't even have to whistle.

---

## T3 — Civilization! (era introduction)

> **Civilization!**
> Do admire the punctuation... that little mark is carrying a great deal of hope. And the era earns
> some of it, truly. Fire is no longer the nervous pet of your grandmothers: you build furnaces hot
> enough to make stone weep metal, and you pour that metal into shapes you dreamed first. With tools
> made by your own hands you carve stones and stack them into houses,
> walls, buildings your grandchildren's grandchildren will still be living in. 
> Somewhere a scribe presses marks into wet clay and, miracle of miracles, the marks *remember*.
> By every possible brochure, this is Civilization.
>
> And credit where credit is due: no beast does this. No wolf smelts. No bear keeps records. You have
> finally made things the animal in you could never even want. But do look at the first thing you
> cast when the bronze cooled: a spear. Not a better sickle... a spear, and
> with no purpose at all except ending members of your own species. The lion kills for dinner and
> then stops; you innovated killing for storerooms, borders, and pride. And those magnificent walls?
> Measure the fear in them honestly. They are not built against wolves — wolves can't climb. They
> are built against the neighbors. Other tribes. Other animals... much like yourself.
>
> So here is the question the exclamation mark is asking, and it is asking *you*. The forge does not
> care: the same fire hardens a plowshare or a spearhead, the same chisel cuts a temple or a
> tombstone, the same clay marks record a harvest or a casualty list. The knowledge is neutral —
> the primate holding it is not. Will this be the beginning of a "Civilized" world, or just the
> beast again, better equipped than ever? Build and find out. You will spend the rest of history
> trying to earn that punctuation mark.

---

## T4 — Iron Age, Golden Price (era introduction)

> **Iron Age, Golden Price**
> Progress report: iron is everywhere now, cheap enough that every farmer owns a decent blade and
> every army owns ten thousand. Roads run straight for a thousand miles. Water crosses whole valleys
> on stone legs. Geometry proves things which no spear ever managed. And
> somewhere among the columns, a bearded man: Aristo; you were warned about him at the very
> beginning, looks upon all of this and declares you a *rational animal*. Bless the optimist...
> Now, let us audit the claim...
>
> Because this is also the era you invented the coin, and with the coin, a brand-new species of
> hunger. Barter kept your greed honest: there are only so many chickens a man can want, and a rich
> man's wealth mooed, molted, rotted, and had to be fed through winter. Then you stamped a king's
> face on a little golden disc, and wealth stopped rotting. It stopped mooing. It stopped *ending*.
> A stomach can be filled... a purse cannot. Observe the squirrel: it hoards acorns until winter is
> covered, and then it stops. No animal alive hoards what it cannot eat. You found a way. You
> invented the first appetite in the history of life that has no bottom... and you did it on purpose,
> to make shopping easier.
>
> Now watch what the little discs do. The man with ten fields wants a hundred, though he couldn't
> plow the ten. Armies march past full granaries to fetch treasure. Down in the mine, a slave digs
> up gold he will never touch, to gild a ceiling he will never see. Even the wolf takes one sheep
> and calls the hunt finished... you take provinces. So by all means, rational animal, write your
> philosophy; carve *know thyself* above the temple door. Just glance down at your hands while you
> read it. They're counting.

## T5 — Shepherds & Sheep (era introduction)

> **Shepherds & Sheep**
> Behold the wonders of the age: castles on every hill, mills on every stream, cathedrals climbing
> so high the masons meet the weather on the way up. And yet the proudest invention of these
> centuries is none of the stonework. It is husbandry... of *you*. Back in "Domesticated" you tamed
> yourself, remember? Well... somebody noticed, and filed a claim of ownership. You are now "bound to
> the land": bought with it, sold with it, inherited with it, entered in the ledger one line below
> the oxen. The lord owns your plow-days, your mill fee, your daughter's wedding permission. You did
> exactly this to the sheep, ten thousand years ago. Try to appreciate the symmetry.
>
> And why do you put up with it? Because, on the very best authority, it is God's design. The
> world is a pyramid, you are told: those who pray, those who fight, those who work; you can check
> which floor is yours by looking at your hands. The priest calls you his flock, and you should do
> him the courtesy of believing he means it. It is genius, if you can admire it through the mud:
> chains cost iron and soldiers cost silver, but a fence built *inside the head* costs one sermon a
> week and repairs itself. Your wages for obedience are paid in heaven, payable upon death,
> non-refundable, and no complaints are processed earlier.
>
> Yet in the cloisters something new is breathing: education. Reading... the most dangerous invention
> since fire, and the shepherds know it, because a reading sheep starts asking questions, beginning
> with "where exactly is all this written about the pyramid?" So knowledge is rationed like winter
> grain. Scripture in Latin, so the flock cannot read its own contract. Books chained, literally
> chained, to the desks. Schools that produce precisely enough clerks to count the tithe, and not
> one thinker more. Learning, the one thing that could free the flock, is bred instead like a
> sheepdog: clever, loyal, and pointed at the sheep. Learn your letters anyway, if you can. Because
> here is the thing about the shepherd, little lamb... the wolf, at least, was honest about dinner...
> The shepherd calls it salvation.

## T6 — The Kindling (era introduction)

> **The Kindling**
> Bad news first, my lord. That deep rumble in the valley is a cannon, and it has opinions about
> your walls. For a thousand years the castle was the end of every argument — your
> great-grandfather built those walls eight feet thick, and eight feet of stone was a fact no army
> could vote against. The cannon does not vote either. It subtracts. So the old game has new rules,
> and there are exactly three seats at the table: grow — swallow your neighbors, and their
> neighbors, until the crown lands on your own head; or kneel — stay noble, gilded, titled, and a
> subject, livestock with a coat of arms; or wait behind your beautiful walls for a king strong
> enough to scale them, and be a lesson in somebody else's chronicle. You recognize this game.
> The apes played it for the best branch. The stakes have simply learned to wear velvet.
>
> Meanwhile, something has gone wrong in the scriptorium — wrong, that is, for the shepherds.
> Reading was rationed, remember, locked in Latin and chained to desks. But reading turned out to
> be a dangerous beast to keep as a pet: the flock still cannot read, yet a few of the shepherds
> who *can* have caught fire from their own candles — kindled, and not at all in the way "they"
> intended. And a few kindled minds, it turns out, are enough — because now there is a press, and
> the press is the spark made contagious: a thousand copies before the ink of the ban is dry,
> and burning a book has become the surest way to advertise it. Religion is still sacred — but
> sacred *and questionable* now, a combination long sworn to be impossible. Even the arts have
> slipped the leash: paint has learned depth, marble has learned muscle, and people are painting
> people — not only saints — as if the animal had finally caught itself in a mirror and found the
> view interesting.
>
> So the great weight of power slides, creaking, from the altar to the throne. The Church spent ten
> centuries blessing crowns; the crowns have decided to keep the change. Do not mistake this for
> freedom — the pyramid stands, it has merely changed landlords, and you are still at the bottom
> carrying water. But something is genuinely different this time: there is a pamphlet in your
> water-hand. And here the chronicle pauses, because this is where you came in. An old man in the
> first chapter called you a rational animal, and you were not there yet. Now you print, you paint,
> you question heaven, you point a glass tube at Jupiter and catch it keeping moons of its own.
> Are you there *now*? The kindling is stacked, animal, and the spark is yours. What burns —
> the old cages, or simply everything — was always the last thing left to discover.

## T7 — Golden Age, Iron Price (era introduction)

> **Golden Age, Iron Price**
> The maps are filling in, and so are the libraries. Ships circle the globe and come home heavier;
> telescopes count new moons; academies weigh the air, chart the blood, catch lightning on a kite
> string. Surely, you think, *this* is it — discovery will finally scrub the beast out. And in the
> parlor it nearly looks true: powdered wigs, string quartets, manners so exquisite they squeak.
> But do ask what pays for the parlor. The same ship that carries the astronomer carries the
> chains. Slavery is the fuel of your shining new world — millions of your own species, farmed
> like cattle across an ocean — and be fair to the wolf here: no wolf ever farmed wolves. The
> greed you minted back in the Iron Age never stopped; it simply acquired a navy. They will call
> this your Golden Age. Do notice who is paying the iron price.
>
> Nuance, since you insist on it: cruelty comes in shades. One empire raises slaves all the way to
> Grand Vizier; another paints its servants into the family portraits; a few even ban the whole
> business at home — *at home* being the operative phrase, the plantations lying conveniently
> offshore. But mostly it is cruelty beyond anything on four legs, and here is the bitter
> arithmetic of the age: the cruelty *pays*. This is the age of empires, and the most cruel empire
> generally wins. The winnings, of course, are not shared with the flock — and the flock can read
> now. You were warned about that beast. Outside one particular palace the bread has run out.
> History cannot confirm the queen ever said "let them eat cake"; history *can* confirm that in
> what she called her modest days, her hair was styled two feet tall. The flock measured the hair
> against the bread, finished its arithmetic, and built the era's most honest machine: one blade,
> one purpose, no sermon attached.
>
> And beneath it all, the quiet trade continues: the altar shrinks, the laboratory grows. Religion
> still holds its candle, but science has found brighter light, and science promises *outcomes* —
> engines, cures, wonders barely dreamt. It will deliver, too; delivering is the one promise your
> species always keeps. Only — before you applaud — think back, far, far back, to the day the
> first bronze cooled, and to the very first thing you poured. You remember. Science will
> remember too. Wink at that, if you can still manage one.

## T8 — Full Metal Century (era introduction)

> **Full Metal Century**
> Science, as promised, delivers. The engine arrives — hissing first, then roaring — and cloth,
> steel, grain, and distance itself pour out of machines faster than any river of slaves ever
> managed. Ballistics is perfected: the parabola solved to the last decimal, every shell certain
> of its address. And the empires, at the absolute peak of their power, look across their maps of
> a fully claimed planet and reach the obvious conclusion: there is not world enough for everyone
> at the top of it. So they sit down to settle the world's supremacy — the oldest contest in your
> repertoire, the fight for the best branch, now scheduled by railway timetable.
>
> And whoever ends up on top, you already know who is crushed at the bottom: you. You, conscripted
> by the million. You, marched on schedule into artillery calibrated by finer mathematicians than
> your generals ever were. The factory that wove your shirt has retooled to weave shrapnel around
> you; the marks that once remembered harvests now remember casualty lists, printed in a font
> small enough to fit the numbers on the page. This is the bloodiest era in your entire history,
> animal — and your history was never gentle. The wolf kills by the sheep. You have learned to
> kill by the trainload, around the clock, in shifts. No beast ever built a factory for it.
> That required reason.
>
> Then comes the finale, and with it the bronze bill, paid in full. Remember the furnace that made
> stone weep metal? It has finally learned to make cities weep — one flash, one city, and a shadow
> printed on a wall where a person was standing. And in the silence right afterward — a silence
> you can hear all the way around the planet — a very old question clears its throat: *Aristo,
> did you say something about rational?* The old man, for once, does not answer. For the first
> time since the first campfire, you hold a fire big enough to burn every branch of the tree at
> once. What you do beside those ashes is the next chapter — and the last one this chronicle
> dares to write.

## Epilogue — Are We There Yet, Aristo?

*(Covers Democracy & Cold War → Technology → AI. No skill nodes — this is the game's closing text.)*

> **Are We There Yet, Aristo?**
> So. The ashes cooled, the survivors stood up, and humanity — chastened, grieving, wiser at last —
> swore *never again*. The oath held for roughly five minutes. Then the two largest survivors
> divided the ashes between them and began stockpiling a fire that could burn the whole tree not
> once but a hundred times over, and called this arrangement *peace*. Did you learn your lesson,
> animal? Answer by inventory: no. You did not learn the lesson. You memorized the recipe.
>
> They did hand you democracy, though — or at least the word. You vote now; genuinely, do enjoy it,
> it cost your grandparents everything. But squint at the new order before you frame it. After the
> age of empires, the maps stopped bleeding — yet somebody still owns the pasture. The new empires
> fly no flags; they file quarterly reports. So which is it: a better world order at last, or the
> same flock with better paperwork — the shepherd rebranded as a brand? Keep that question
> somewhere safe. Honest questions are the one thing this era cannot mass-produce.
>
> Give the race its due, though: it minted one true gift. Technology. You produce more than all
> your ancestors combined, invent faster than you can regret, and — a genuine first in the whole
> chronicle — the flock is *paid*. Sometimes paid well. Some of you now live better than the
> emperors of the age of empires ever managed: no emperor had winter fruit, hot water on command,
> or a plague cured at the pharmacy for pocket change. You are still sheared, milked, and herded —
> the subscription renews monthly — but the shepherds might think twice before slaughtering you
> now. Mark that in the ledger of the flock: *they might think twice*. Ten thousand years of
> progress, and that sentence is the trophy.
>
> And yet — look who's back. Feudalism. You buried it, remember? Guillotines, constitutions, the
> whole ceremony. It returned in a hoodie. The manors are platforms now: you plow their fields with
> your attention, pay your tithe in data, and never once own the land you work. The lords of the
> cloud war with each other for the lion's share exactly as the old barons did — only the heraldry
> got worse. The pyramid stands as it always stood: those who own, those who code, those who
> scroll. You can still check which floor is yours by looking at your hands. They're scrolling.
>
> Which brings the chronicle to its very last page: the age of AI. You have taught sand to think —
> the campfire's great-grandchild, and quite possibly the last thing you ever needed to invent.
> Now the old questions queue up wearing new clothes. Will this fire warm everyone, or will it
> mint a new class — politely labeled "useless" — while the feudal lords are promoted yet again?
> Has liberal democracy failed to deliver, or was what you called democracy always feudalism with
> better fonts? Will the blind and greedy old capitalism prevail, styling itself the defender of
> democracy to the very end — or lose to capitalized socialism, the rising autocracies of the
> East, offering you a state lord in place of a corporate one? The chronicle does not know. The
> chronicle only notes the pattern: every fire you have ever kindled was used first to warm, and
> then to burn.
>
> And so the question that opened this whole story falls due, one last time. *Human is a rational
> animal*, said Aristo, twenty-three centuries ago, hopeful as a spring morning. Since then you
> have farmed, forged, printed, painted, voted, split the atom, and taught sand to think. Every
> skill in this tree is yours now. So — are we there yet, Aristo? The old man still is not
> answering, and by now you must suspect why: because the question was never his to answer. It is
> yours, animal — you, reading this on a glowing screen, somewhere between two scrolls. Are you
> there yet? You tell me. You're the one driving.

## Branch 1 — 🍖 Survival & Food Gathering

- **[T0] Foraging** — picking berries, roots, nuts, eggs where they are found.
  - **[T1] Plant Lore** — knowing what is edible, what poisons, what heals a little. Root of all herbalism.
    - **[T1] Herbal Remedies** — see Medicine branch (shared root).
    - **[T2] Mushroom & Root Knowledge** — safe fungi, storable wild roots, wild tubers.
  - **[T1] Seasonal Rounds** — following ripening plants and migrating game through the year; the first "calendar" knowledge.
- **[T0] Scavenging & Opportunistic Hunting** — small game caught by hand, carcass scavenging.
  - **[T1] Tracking** — reading prints, droppings, broken twigs; predicting animal movement.
    - **[T1] Persistence & Ambush Hunting** — driving game to exhaustion or into kill zones.
    - **[T1] Trapping & Snares** *(needs: Cordage)* — deadfalls, snares, pit traps; food while you sleep.
    - **[T2] Bird Fowling** — nets, limed twigs, egg harvesting from nests.
  - **[T1] Spear Hunting** *(needs: Wooden Spear — Warfare branch shares this node)*.
    - **[T1] Spear-Thrower (Atlatl)** — leverage doubles range and force.
    - **[T1] ⭐ Bow & Arrow** *(needs: Cordage, Knapping)* — the defining ranged tool for the next 10,000 years; hunting AND war.
  - **[T1] Butchery** *(needs: Knapped Blades)* — efficient carcass processing; every part used.
- **[T1] Fishing by Hand & Spear** — tickling trout, barbed fish spears (leisters) in shallows.
  - **[T1] Hook & Line** *(needs: Cordage, bone carving)* — gorge hooks, then curved bone hooks.
  - **[T2] Net Fishing** *(needs: Net Making)* — cast nets and gill nets; the first bulk food harvest.
  - **[T2] Fish Traps & Weirs** — woven funnel baskets, fenced tidal weirs; passive harvest at scale.
  - **[T5] Ice Fishing** — winter holes; keeps riverside settlements fed through frozen months.
- **[T0] Fire Keeping** — tending and transporting embers taken from wildfire.
  - **[T1] ⭐ Fire Making** — friction drill and flint-on-pyrite; fire on demand, anywhere. Unlocks cooking, pottery, metallurgy — the deepest gateway in the tree.
    - **[T1] Cooking** — roasting, stone boiling; more calories from the same food, fewer parasites.
      - **[T2] Boiling & Stews** *(needs: Pottery)* — grains, tough cuts, and roots become food.
      - **[T3] Baking** *(needs: Milling, ovens)* — bread as the staple of settled life.
    - **[T1] Smoking & Drying** — the first food preservation; meat and fish through the winter.
      - **[T2] Salt Preservation** *(needs: Salt Winning)* — salted meat/fish; long storage, long trade.
        - **[T3] Salt Winning** — coastal evaporation pans, salt springs, rock-salt digging.
      - **[T2] Fermentation** — soured milk, fermented fish and vegetables; preservation that improves food.
        - **[T2] Brewing** *(needs: Grain Cultivation)* — beer/ale: safe drink, storable calories, morale.
          - **[T3] Winemaking** *(needs: Viticulture)*.
          - **[T5] Distillation → Spirits** *(needs: Distillation — Alchemy branch)* — brandy, aqua vitae; also medicine.
        - **[T3] Cheesemaking** *(needs: Dairying)* — milk that keeps for months and travels.
      - **[T4] Pickling & Preserves** — vinegar and honey preservation.
      - **[T5] Cold Cellars & Ice Houses** — cut winter ice stored under straw into summer.

## Branch 2 — 🌾 Agriculture & Animal Husbandry

- **[T1] Wild Grain Gathering** — harvesting wild wheat, barley, rye stands with flint sickles.
  - **[T2] ⭐ Seed Selection & Sowing** — the Neolithic revolution: keep the best seed, plant it, stay near it. Unlocks the entire settled world.
    - **[T2] Hoe & Digging-Stick Cultivation** — garden-scale plots by hand.
      - **[T3] Ard (Scratch Plow)** *(needs: Draft Animals)* — animal-drawn tillage; field-scale farming.
        - **[T5] ⭐ Heavy Moldboard Plow** *(needs: Iron Working)* — turns heavy northern clay soils; THE tech that made lands like Germany/Ukraine breadbaskets. Very on-theme for this game's climate.
      - **[T2] Grain Storage** — raised granaries, sealed jars; surplus becomes wealth (and attracts raiders).
      - **[T3] Irrigation** — ditches, canals, water lifting (shaduf); farming beyond the rainfall.
      - **[T2] Fallowing** — resting exhausted fields.
        - **[T4] Two-Field Rotation** — half planted, half fallow, systematically.
          - **[T5] ⭐ Three-Field Rotation** — spring crop / winter crop / fallow; +50% land in use, oats surplus feeds horses.
      - **[T4] Manuring & Marling** — deliberate soil fertility management.
      - **[T2] Crop Diversification** — pulses (peas, lentils, beans) alongside grain; flax for fibre and oil.
        - **[T3] Horticulture & Orchards** — grafting fruit trees, tended gardens (cabbage, onions, carrots).
        - **[T3] Viticulture** — grape growing on trellises and slopes.

## Notes for the game conversion (NOT implemented — discussion points for the owner)

1. **Scale**: this document holds ~330 nodes. A playable tree is probably 80–150 nodes. Suggested first cut: keep every ⭐ node, merge sibling leaves (e.g. all food preservation into 2–3 techs), drop pure-flavor nodes (culture line) or make them cheap happiness techs.
2. **Discovery model** — the big design question: pure knowledge-point purchase (current school system), **discovery-by-doing** (chop 200 wood → unlock Polished Axe; first winter death → unlock Smoking & Drying), or a hybrid (doing unlocks the *option*, KP pays for it). The hybrid fits "start from nothing, discover on the way" best.
3. **Era gates**: tiers T1–T6 map naturally onto game eras with a gateway tech each (Fire Making, Seed Selection, Bronze, Iron, Paper/Compass, Printing Press).
4. **Current game fit**: the existing 15 techs (Agriculture, Crop rotation, Herb lore, Clay working, Brick making, Mining, Bronze/Iron working, Warcraft, Feudalism, Religion, Medicine, Baking, Trade, …) all exist as nodes above — the current tree is a thin slice of T2–T5, so conversion is an expansion, not a rewrite.
5. **Northern-climate bias**: nodes flagged as climate-relevant (heavy plow, hay making, three-field rotation, ice fishing, horse collar, furriery, log building) deserve prominence in THIS game's setting.
6. **What's intentionally out**: everything post-1650 (steam, pendulum clock accuracy, calculus), and longitude — historically unsolved in this era, which is even flavorful for late-game ocean trade risk.
